The arguments

1. It fails our release bar. Evidence: The hallucination rate is 9 of 20 answers. That is nine times our 5% hard-gate limit, and a hard-gate failure blocks the merge.
2. Fixing hallucinations alone won't clear our quality bar. Evidence: Two more of the 20 answers failed for other reasons: 1 on robustness and 1 on UX trust. Both are P1 risks.
3. We can afford to measure the fix. Evidence: The $85,000 hallucination eval fits inside our $200,000 quarterly cap, even alongside the $70,000 robustness eval. Engineering removes the hallucinations, and this eval tells us whether the fix worked.

Twenty answers is a small sample, but the gap is too wide to be chance. To get under 5% on 20 answers we would need zero hallucinations, and we got 9. Insights

What I learned

Where the course was hardest, what you’ll keep doing, and the moment your thinking changed.

Friction

The hardest part was matching each failure to the right gate (hard, soft or advisory) and the right check (rule check, safety rule or LLM judge). I worked through it by asking what each failure costs the business. Hallucination damages trust and the brand, so it became a hard gate that blocks the merge. Latency only slows answers down, so it became a soft gate where the PM can sign off on an override. Slang in the tone was minor, so it became an advisory gate that only posts a warning. Writing out each gate's pass rule also caught a mistake: my latency threshold was written backwards.

Key learnings

At work, I'll start any AI governance review the same way: audit a small set of real answers, tag each failure by type, and set a release threshold before anything ships. I'll also budget evals by risk. The most expensive online evals go to P0 failures like hallucination, and cheaper code checks or periodic audits cover the rest. When I bring a recommendation to leadership, I'll lead with the decision and back every argument with a number.

Aha moment

In my audit, 9 of 20 Ascend IQ answers invented information. For example, it named speakers as confirmed when they had only been invited, were tentative or had declined. It gave confident answers rather than admitting it didn't know. That changed how I think about evals: you can't trust an AI product until you've measured how often it makes things up.
